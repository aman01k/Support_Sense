# Part 2 — Dataset (≈ 2.5 minutes)

**Goal:** the examiner understands which data we used, why we chose it, what it looks like, and how it shaped the design.
**Speaker:** Aman presents all four slides; change this if you split it differently.
Slides 7–10 of the SupportSense Viva Presentation deck; the same text is in the speaker notes.

---

## Slide 7 — Choosing a dataset · ~40 s
**Show:** comparison table, Bitext vs Tobi-Bueck, plus the "data breach labelled medium" callout.

**Say:**
> To train a classifier we needed customer messages with correct labels. We compared two public datasets from Hugging Face.
>
> The **Tobi-Bueck** dataset looked attractive because it has priority labels. But more than half of it is German, and its labels are noisy. Our quick baseline model reached only **52%** on department and **62%** on priority. For example, a ticket titled *"Urgent Help Needed for Possible Data Breach"* is labelled **medium** priority.
>
> The **Bitext** dataset is English-only, has no missing values, and the same baseline reached **99.7%**. So we chose Bitext.

---

## Slide 8 — What the data looks like · ~35 s
**Show:** five real rows (message → category) and four key numbers.

**Say:**
> Bitext has 26,872 customer messages. We use two columns: the **message**, which is our input, and the **category**, which is the label we predict.
>
> Messages are short, about 9 words on average. There are no missing values, but there are 2,237 duplicates, which we remove in the next step.
>
> The data also has realistic noise: typos like "rtying", slang like "wanna", and placeholders like *{{Order Number}}*. We keep the typos because real customers make them, and we remove the placeholders.

---

## Slide 9 — 11 categories, not balanced · ~40 s
**Show:** bar chart of the 11 categories, with ACCOUNT and CANCEL highlighted, and how we handled the imbalance.

**Say:**
> There are 11 categories. They're **not balanced**: ACCOUNT has almost 6,000 examples, while CANCEL has only 950, about **six times fewer**.
>
> If we ignored this, the model could favour the big classes. So we did three things: a **stratified** train/test split, so every category keeps its share; **balanced class weights** in the model; and we report **macro F1**, which treats all 11 categories equally.
>
> One detail: in this dataset, CANCEL means questions about cancellation *fees*. Cancelling an order belongs to ORDER.

---

## Slide 10 — What the dataset doesn't give us · ~35 s
**Show:** dark slide; category is labelled, sentiment and urgency are not; the technique chosen for each.

**Say:**
> Bitext gives us category labels, but **no sentiment labels and no urgency labels**. Without labels we can't train a supervised model.
>
> That decided our design: **category** is a trained machine learning model; **sentiment** uses VADER, a pre-trained analyser; and **urgency** uses business rules.
>
> One honest point: Bitext is generated from templates, so it's cleaner than real tickets. That's why our accuracy is very high, and we'll come back to it in the limitations.

## Likely questions on this part
| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Is the data real? | It's synthetic: generated by Bitext from templates, with typos and slang added. |
| Why not label urgency yourselves? | It would mean labelling thousands of messages by hand, and the one dataset with priority labels showed how noisy such labels get. |
| Why Hugging Face and not Kaggle? | It downloads with no login, and both datasets we compared were there. |
| What are intents? | Bitext also has 27 finer intents (e.g. `cancel_order` inside ORDER). We used the 11 categories because they match support teams. |
